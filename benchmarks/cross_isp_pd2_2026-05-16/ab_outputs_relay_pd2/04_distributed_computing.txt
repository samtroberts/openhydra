**The Architecture of Scale: Distributed Computing**

In the modern digital age, the concept of "distributed computing" has evolved from a niche academic curiosity into the bedrock of how the global internet functions. Originating in the 1960s, the term likely stems from the idea of considering a computer system as a collection of smaller, independent machines rather than a monolithic, centralized server. Today, distributed computing refers to the practice of coordinating multiple computers, often located in different physical locations, to work together as a single, unified system.

The primary objective of distributed computing is to overcome the limitations of traditional, centralized architectures. Centralized systems, though powerful, are prone to single points of failure; if one server crashes, the entire service can come down. In contrast, a distributed system is designed for resilience and scalability. By replicating data and processing tasks across multiple nodes, these systems can continue operating even when individual components fail, ensuring high availability and reliability.

Furthermore, distributed computing enables massive scalability and resource utilization. As hardware prices plummet and bandwidth increases, organizations can offload non-critical workloads to secondary machines, allowing primary servers to focus on core tasks.
